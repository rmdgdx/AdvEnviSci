Course syllabus · ENVS 601

Advanced Environmental Science

Six chapters over twelve weeks, two sessions each. The course moves from how environmental science frames a problem, to how the earth system and its element cycles actually behave, to the formal tools — impact assessment, risk analysis, resilience theory — used to decide what should be done.

Credit3 units · 3 hrs/week
LevelGraduate (MS / PhD)
Structure6 chapters × 2 weeks
FormatSeminar + computing lab
PrerequisitesGeneral chemistry, statistics, intro ecology

Course description

This course treats environmental science as a quantitative, spatially explicit discipline that has to produce defensible answers under uncertainty. Students first establish the framing: what the field studies, how systems thinking structures the questions, and how the earth's physical subsystems interact. They then learn to represent that system spatially through GIS and remote sensing, to follow matter and energy through it via biogeochemical and ecosystem analysis, and to model the reactive chemistry of the atmosphere.

The final third turns from description to decision. Environmental impact assessment and risk analysis supply the formal procedures for anticipating harm; ecosystem dynamics and resilience theory supply the reason those procedures often fail — thresholds, hysteresis and regime shifts do not respect linear impact predictions.

Sessions are seminar-style. Roughly half of each meeting is worked calculation, spatial analysis or data interpretation; the rest is critical reading of primary literature. Students are expected to argue with the readings rather than summarise them.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the term, a student who has done the work should be able to do the following.

  1. Frame a problem systemically. Describe an environmental issue in terms of stocks, flows, feedbacks and boundaries, and state explicitly what the chosen system boundary leaves out.
  2. Work spatially. Build a georeferenced dataset, run a suitability or change-detection analysis in a GIS, and produce a map that survives technical scrutiny.
  3. Quantify a cycle. Construct a carbon, nitrogen or water budget for a defined system, check it for closure, and identify which flux the conclusion is most sensitive to.
  4. Reason chemically about the atmosphere. Derive lifetimes from rate data, distinguish NOx-limited from VOC-limited ozone regimes, and evaluate whether a proposed emission control would work.
  5. Conduct an assessment. Carry out screening, scoping and impact prediction for a real project, and produce a health or ecological risk characterisation with its uncertainty stated.
  6. Diagnose resilience. Identify thresholds and alternative stable states in a system, interpret early warning indicators, and design a management or restoration response.
  7. Write for two audiences. Produce a technically rigorous paper and a short policy brief on the same problem, each pitched correctly for its reader.

Chapters and weekly coverage

Each chapter runs two sessions. The first establishes the framework; the second applies it to data, a model or a real site. Subtopics below are the benchmark coverage — they are what the corresponding lecture page should contain.

1

Overview of Environmental Science

Weeks 1–2

Establishes the intellectual frame: what the field claims to know, how it reasons about coupled systems, and the ethical and legal architecture within which environmental decisions get made.

Week 1Foundations and systems thinking
  • Environmental science as an interdisciplinary field; scope and limits of the scientific method applied to open systems
  • Historical paradigms: conservation and preservation, environmentalism, sustainability science, the planetary boundaries framework
  • Systems thinking: stocks and flows, positive and negative feedback, mass balance, residence time, steady state
  • Environmental ethics — anthropocentric, biocentric, ecocentric positions; environmental justice; indigenous and local knowledge systems
Week 2Drivers, data and the policy frame
  • Drivers of environmental change: population, affluence, technology; the IPAT and Kaya identities; ecological footprint accounting
  • The triple planetary crisis — climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution — and the couplings between them
  • Environmental data: measurement scales, units, precision and accuracy, uncertainty, indicators and composite indices
  • Governance: multilateral environmental agreements, the SDGs, and the national legal framework for air, water, waste and EIA

Due: diagnostic problem set (ungraded, Week 2) · position paper on a contested environmental claim, 1,500 words, end of Week 2

2

Earth Systems and GIS

Weeks 3–4

Couples the physical earth system to the spatial analysis skills every later chapter assumes. By the end, students can locate a process, not just describe it.

Week 3The coupled earth system
  • Atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere, biosphere and cryosphere as interacting subsystems; timescales of coupling
  • Solar radiation and the energy balance; atmospheric general circulation; ocean circulation and the thermohaline conveyor
  • Plate tectonics, the rock cycle, physical and chemical weathering, geomorphic process, soil formation and classification
  • The hydrologic cycle; watersheds as the working unit of analysis; groundwater–surface water interaction
Week 4GIS, remote sensing and spatial analysis
  • Spatial data models: vector and raster; coordinate systems, projections, datums and transformations; resolution and scale
  • Data acquisition: GNSS field survey, LiDAR, UAV imagery, and satellite platforms including Landsat and Sentinel
  • Core analysis: overlay, buffering, proximity, spatial interpolation, DEM and terrain derivatives, multi-criteria suitability modelling
  • Remote sensing workflows: spectral indices, supervised and unsupervised classification, accuracy assessment, change detection
  • Cartographic communication, metadata standards, and the honest representation of spatial uncertainty

Due: GIS laboratory — a suitability or change-detection analysis with map output and written method, end of Week 4

3

Biogeochemistry and Ecosystem Ecology

Weeks 5–6

Follows matter and energy through living systems, and establishes how ecosystem fluxes are actually measured rather than assumed.

Week 5Biogeochemical cycles
  • The cycle framework: reservoirs, fluxes, turnover and residence time; stable isotopes as tracers of pathway and source
  • Carbon: terrestrial and oceanic pools, GPP, NPP and NEP, soil organic carbon, blue carbon, the anthropogenic perturbation
  • Nitrogen and phosphorus: fixation and denitrification, the Haber–Bosch perturbation, the nitrogen cascade, eutrophication and hypoxia
  • Sulfur, water and trace metal cycles; redox conditions as the master control on element mobility in soils and sediments
Week 6Ecosystem structure and function
  • Energy flow and trophic structure; ecological efficiency, food web architecture, top-down and bottom-up control
  • Controls on primary production; decomposition, mineralisation, and nutrient limitation across biomes
  • Ecological stoichiometry; ecosystem services, natural capital, and the limits of monetary valuation
  • Measurement and modelling: eddy covariance flux towers, long-term ecological research networks, ecosystem simulation models

Due: problem set 1 — construct and audit a biogeochemical budget · midterm examination covering Chapters 1–3, end of Week 6

4

Atmospheric Chemistry

Weeks 7–8

The compartment with the shortest reaction timescales and the most direct link between chemistry and regulation.

Week 7Composition, structure and tropospheric photochemistry
  • Vertical structure, lapse rate and stability; the boundary layer, mixing height and ventilation
  • Trace gas budgets, mixing ratios, chemical lifetime and spatial variability; the atmospheric oxidising capacity
  • Actinic flux and photolysis rates; the hydroxyl radical budget; peroxy radicals and PAN as a reservoir species
  • NOx–VOC–ozone chemistry, ozone isopleth diagrams, urban and regional smog regimes, and the design of a control strategy
Week 8Aerosols, deposition, stratosphere and climate
  • Aerosol nucleation, condensation and coagulation; size modes; secondary organic aerosol; aerosol–cloud interaction
  • PM2.5 composition and exposure, monitoring network design, ambient standards and the air quality index
  • Dry and wet deposition, acid deposition and critical loads, long-range transboundary transport
  • Stratospheric ozone: the Chapman cycle, catalytic halogen destruction, polar stratospheric clouds, the Montreal Protocol
  • Greenhouse gases, radiative forcing, GWP, short-lived climate pollutants, and air-quality–climate co-benefits

Due: problem set 2 — lifetimes, photochemistry and an ozone regime diagnosis · air quality data laboratory using a real monitoring dataset, end of Week 8

5

Environmental Impact Assessment and Risk Analysis

Weeks 9–10

The formal machinery for predicting harm before a decision is made, and for quantifying it with its uncertainty declared.

Week 9The EIA process
  • Origins and rationale of EIA; the Philippine EIS System, ECC and CNC requirements, and comparable international regimes
  • Screening and scoping; baseline characterisation; definition and honest treatment of project alternatives
  • Impact prediction and evaluation: Leopold matrices, network and checklist methods, GIS overlay, and predictive modelling
  • Significance criteria, the mitigation hierarchy, environmental management and monitoring plans
  • Public participation and consultation; strategic environmental assessment; cumulative and transboundary impacts
Week 10Risk assessment and risk-based decisions
  • Human health risk assessment: hazard identification, dose–response, exposure assessment, risk characterisation
  • Ecological risk assessment: assessment endpoints, conceptual models, hazard quotients, species sensitivity distributions
  • Uncertainty and variability: sensitivity analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, and communicating confidence in an output
  • Disaster risk as the interaction of hazard, exposure and vulnerability; climate risk screening
  • Risk communication, risk perception, the precautionary principle, and how risk actually enters a decision

Due: EIA and risk portfolio — screening and scoping report plus a quantitative risk characterisation for one real project, end of Week 10 · capstone proposal due Week 10

6

Advanced Ecosystem Dynamics and Resilience

Weeks 11–12

Why some systems absorb a disturbance and others cross a threshold and stay there — and what that implies for everything assessed in Chapter 5.

Week 11Dynamics, thresholds and regime shifts
  • Succession, disturbance regimes, patch dynamics, and the shift away from equilibrium ecology
  • Population and community models: logistic growth, predator–prey, competition, metapopulation and source–sink dynamics
  • Stability, resistance and recovery; alternative stable states, hysteresis, thresholds and tipping points
  • Documented regime shifts: shallow lakes, coral reefs, drylands, forest–savanna transitions, fisheries collapse
Week 12Resilience and adaptive governance
  • Engineering versus ecological resilience; the adaptive cycle and panarchy across scales
  • Early warning signals of critical transitions: critical slowing down, rising variance and autocorrelation, flickering
  • Social–ecological systems; commons governance; adaptive management and adaptive co-management
  • Restoration ecology, rewilding, assisted migration, and climate adaptation planning
  • Designing for resilience at landscape scale, including protected area and corridor networks

Due: capstone paper and policy brief, presented in seminar, end of Week 12

Chapters 3 and 5 carry the heaviest load by design: the midterm closes the descriptive half of the course while it is still fresh, and the assessment portfolio in Week 10 is deliberately placed before the resilience material, so that Chapter 6 can be taught as a critique of what the students have just produced.

Capstone paper and policy brief25%

6,000–8,000 words plus a two-page brief on the same problem, presented in the final seminar. Proposal due Week 10.

GIS laboratory and map portfolio15%

Week 4. Graded on method and cartographic honesty as much as on the result.

EIA and risk portfolio15%

Week 10. A real project, screened and scoped, with a quantitative risk characterisation.

Problem sets and data laboratory15%

Three quantitative sets plus the Week 8 air quality data lab. Collaboration allowed; written solutions must be your own.

Midterm examination15%

Week 6, covering Chapters 1–3. Formula sheet provided; no memorisation of constants.

Seminar participation and literature leads15%

Each student leads discussion on two primary papers and submits the Week 2 position paper.

Course policies

Late work

Problem sets lose 10% per day up to three days; after that they are graded for feedback only. Extensions are granted for documented reasons, requested before the deadline rather than after it.

Computation and software

All quantitative and spatial work must be reproducible. Any tool is acceptable — QGIS, R, Python, Google Earth Engine, or a spreadsheet — provided the submission includes inputs, method, and enough annotation that another student could rerun it.

Use of AI tools

Permitted for code debugging, language editing and literature discovery; disclose it in a short note at the end of the submission. Not permitted for generating analysis, interpretation or argument, which is the graded part of the work. Undisclosed use is treated as an academic integrity violation.

Academic integrity

Fabricated or selectively trimmed data is the most serious offence available in this field and is treated as such. Cite the source of every dataset, basemap and image used.

Fieldwork and accessibility

Accommodations are arranged through the graduate office; contact the instructor in the first two weeks so that field and laboratory components can be planned properly.

Benchmark texts and references

Chapter coverage above is benchmarked against these standard graduate treatments. No single book is required; chapters are assigned per session.

Chapters 1–2

  • Chapin, Matson & Vitousek — Principles of Terrestrial Ecosystem Ecology, for the systems framing.
  • Kump, Kasting & Crane — The Earth System.
  • Steffen et al. — planetary boundaries literature.
  • Longley et al. — Geographic Information Science and Systems; Jensen — Remote Sensing of the Environment.

Chapters 3–4

  • Schlesinger & Bernhardt — Biogeochemistry: An Analysis of Global Change.
  • Chapin et al. — ecosystem ecology, energy and nutrient chapters.
  • Jacob — Introduction to Atmospheric Chemistry; Seinfeld & Pandis — Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics.
  • Finlayson-Pitts & Pitts for mechanistic depth; IPCC AR6 WG1 and the WMO/UNEP Ozone Assessment for current numbers.

Chapters 5–6

  • Glasson, Therivel & Chadwick — Introduction to Environmental Impact Assessment.
  • Canter — Environmental Impact Assessment; PD 1586 and DENR EIS System procedural manuals.
  • US EPA risk assessment guidelines — human health and ecological frameworks.
  • Scheffer — Critical Transitions in Nature and Society; Gunderson & Holling — Panarchy; Ostrom — Governing the Commons.
