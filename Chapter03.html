    Biogeochemistry & Ecosystem Ecology

    How elements and energy move through living systems — and how scientists measure them.

    A carbon atom may spend centuries inside soil, days inside a leaf, decades inside the ocean, or millions of years locked inside rock. What determines where it goes next?

    The answer is never the atom itself. It is the rate of each pathway available to it — and rates are what this chapter teaches you to find, calculate and interpret. Ecosystem ecology studies the interactions among organisms, energy, nutrients, the atmosphere, the hydrosphere, soils, sediments and climate, treating them as one coupled system rather than as separate subjects.

    Two weeks, two halves of a single argument. Week 5 follows the elements: where they are stored, how fast they move, and what happens when humans change a flux. Week 6 follows the energy and the organisms: how production is controlled, how matter is recycled, and how any of it is actually measured in the field.

    Week 5

    Biogeochemical cycles

    Earth continually recycles matter — but if matter is recycled, why can ecosystems still suffer nutrient shortages or pollution?

    Because recycling is a rate, not a guarantee. An element can be abundant in one reservoir and unavailable in another; it can be released faster than it is removed, or removed faster than it is supplied. The word itself names the three sciences you need at once:

    BIO

    Living organisms — the catalysts. Most environmentally important transformations are microbially mediated and would be impossibly slow without enzymes.

    GEO

    Earth materials — rock, soil, sediment, water and air. These set what is available, where it is stored, and for how long.

    CHEMISTRY

    Transformations of elements between chemical forms. Form decides solubility, mobility, bioavailability and toxicity.

    A biogeochemical cycle is the circulation of an element through the biosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere and lithosphere. Different elements take radically different routes, and the differences are the point: carbon has a large, fast atmospheric reservoir, nitrogen has an enormous but chemically inert one, and phosphorus has essentially none at all. Those three facts explain most of what follows.

    Lesson 5.1 — Reservoirs, fluxes and residence times

    Every quantitative statement in this chapter rests on four terms. Learn them precisely now and the rest of the week is bookkeeping.

    Reservoir / pool

    A place where an element is stored: atmosphere, vegetation, soil, ocean, sediment, rock.

    Stock

    The quantity held in a reservoir. Units of mass: Pg C, Tg N, mol.

    Flux

    The rate of movement between reservoirs. Units of mass per time: Pg C yr⁻¹.

    Source / sink

    A reservoir that is net-losing to, or net-gaining from, the one you are studying.

    Residence time

    τ = M / Fout Stock divided by output flux. Check the units every time: Pg C ÷ (Pg C yr⁻¹) = yr. If your answer does not come out in time units, the arithmetic is wrong regardless of how reasonable the number looks.

    Residence time is the average duration a unit of material spends in a reservoir. It is also the reservoir's time constant: after a change in loading, roughly 63% of the adjustment happens within one τ, 86% within two and 95% within three. That makes τ a policy quantity — it tells you how long after a successful intervention people should expect to see improvement.

    Worked example — do this before clicking

    A carbon reservoir holds 600 Pg C. Carbon leaves it at 60 Pg C yr⁻¹. What is the residence time?

    Step 1. Identify stock and output flux: M = 600 Pg C, Fout = 60 Pg C yr⁻¹.

    Step 2. τ = 600 ÷ 60 = 10 years.

    Interpretation. Material turns over roughly ten times a century. A pollutant entering this reservoir would be ~95% flushed about 30 years after the source stopped — provided no slower reservoir takes it up first.

    Three for you

    Think like an ecosystem ecologist

    You measure high soil carbon in two forests. Can you conclude that both currently remove large amounts of CO₂ from the atmosphere?

    No. Stock and flux are different quantities. A large pool records the history of accumulation; it says nothing about the current rate. A mature forest can hold enormous carbon while sitting at NEP ≈ 0. Confusing the two is the single most common error in carbon-offset claims.

    Lesson 5.2 — Stable isotopes as biogeochemical tracers

    Elements come in isotopes — atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Stable isotopes do not decay, so their relative abundance is preserved as material moves. Because physical and biological processes discriminate slightly between light and heavy isotopes, each process leaves a fingerprint. This is fractionation, and it turns isotope ratios into a record of where material has been and what happened to it.

    ¹²C / ¹³C

    Carbon source and photosynthetic pathway. C₃ and C₄ plants fractionate differently; fossil carbon is distinctly depleted in ¹³C.

    ¹⁴N / ¹⁵N

    Nitrogen source and trophic position. δ¹⁵N rises by roughly 3–4‰ per trophic level in many systems.

    ¹⁶O / ¹⁸O

    Water origin, evaporation history, temperature — the basis of palaeoclimate reconstruction from ice and carbonate.

    ¹H / ²H

    Deuterium travels with ¹⁸O in the water cycle; together they define the meteoric water line used to identify water sources.

    Ratios are reported in delta notation — the sample's ratio compared with an international standard, expressed in parts per thousand (‰). Conceptually: a negative δ¹³C means the sample is depleted in the heavy isotope relative to the standard; a positive value means it is enriched. You do not need the algebra to reason with it, though it is one line and sits in the box below.

    Go deeper — delta notation and fractionation

    δ (‰) = [(Rsample / Rstandard) − 1] × 1000, where R is the heavy-to-light ratio, e.g. ¹³C/¹²C. The standards are international reference materials; for carbon, values are reported relative to the Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite scale.

    Fractionation arises because bonds involving the lighter isotope are slightly weaker and lighter molecules diffuse slightly faster. Enzymes amplify the effect: Rubisco discriminates strongly against ¹³CO₂, which is why C₃ plant tissue is markedly depleted relative to atmospheric CO₂, and why the C₄ pathway — which concentrates CO₂ before fixation — leaves a distinctly less depleted signature.

    Two cautions. Mixing models assume you know every end member; an unrecognised source invalidates the partitioning. And fractionation factors are temperature- and rate-dependent, so a value measured in one system should not be transplanted to another without justification.

    Lesson 5.3 — The carbon cycle

    Carbon is the element that couples ecosystems to climate, because its principal atmospheric forms — CO₂ and CH₄ — are radiatively active. The cycle has a fast biological loop measured in years to decades and a slow geological loop measured in hundreds of thousands to millions of years. Human activity has done something specific and unusual: it has moved carbon from the slow loop into the fast one, at a rate the slow loop cannot match.

    Explore the full carbon cycle diagram

    Every arrow — photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, air–sea exchange, sedimentation, weathering, volcanism, combustion — is clickable, with an explanation of the process and its approximate magnitude.

    Primary production: GPP, Ra, NPP

    Gross primary production (GPP) is the total carbon fixed by photosynthesis. Producers immediately spend part of it on their own metabolism — autotrophic respiration (Ra). What remains is net primary production (NPP), the carbon actually available for growth, reproduction, and every consumer in the system.

    NPP = GPP − Ra Typical units: g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ for an ecosystem, Pg C yr⁻¹ globally. NPP is the ecological budget line — it bounds everything the rest of the food web can possibly obtain.

    Net ecosystem production

    Consumers and decomposers respire too. Ecosystem respiration (Re) is the sum of autotrophic and heterotrophic respiration, and the difference between fixation and total respiration is net ecosystem production (NEP).

    NEP = GPP − Re   where   Re = Ra + Rh Positive NEP: the ecosystem is accumulating carbon over the measurement period — a sink. Negative NEP: it is losing carbon — a source.
    Terminology caution

    Carbon-accounting vocabulary varies between disciplines and instruments. Eddy covariance reports net ecosystem exchange (NEE), usually with the opposite sign convention to NEP (negative NEE = uptake by the ecosystem). Some authors reserve NBP (net biome production) for budgets that also subtract fire, harvest and lateral export. Always state the convention you are using before you report a number.

    Soil carbon

    Soil is the largest actively exchanging terrestrial carbon reservoir — larger than vegetation and atmosphere combined. It is fed by litter, root exudates and root turnover, and drained by microbial decomposition. The balance depends on temperature, moisture, oxygen availability, substrate chemistry, soil texture and mineral surfaces, and land use.

    The simple chain — and why it is only a first approximation

    Temperature ↑ → microbial activity ↑ → decomposition ↑ → CO₂ release ↑.

    Real soils complicate every arrow. Warming can dry a soil until moisture, not temperature, limits microbes. Carbon bound to mineral surfaces or occluded in aggregates is physically protected regardless of temperature. Microbial communities acclimate. And fresh carbon inputs can accelerate the decomposition of old carbon — the priming effect. Treat the chain as a hypothesis to be tested, not a law.

    Go deeper — what actually stabilises soil organic matter

    The older view attributed persistence to the intrinsic chemistry of "humic substances". Current understanding emphasises ecosystem properties instead: association with mineral surfaces and metal oxides, occlusion inside aggregates, and physical inaccessibility to enzymes. Persistence is therefore a property of the soil environment rather than of the molecule, which is why land-use change — ploughing, which breaks aggregates and exposes protected carbon — can release carbon that had been stable for centuries.

    Blue carbon

    Mangroves, seagrass meadows and salt marshes store carbon out of proportion to their area. Three features combine: they trap sediment from land and water, their soils are waterlogged and largely anoxic, and decomposition under anoxia is slow. The result is carbon accumulating in sediment for centuries to millennia rather than being respired within years.

    Case panel — why mangroves matter beyond coastal protection

    Philippine mangroves are usually defended in public argument on storm-surge grounds alone. That undersells them, and the connections run in both directions:

    • Carbon. Sediment storage per hectare is high relative to most terrestrial forest, and it is lost quickly on conversion.
    • Fisheries. Root structure provides nursery habitat for juveniles of species harvested offshore, so mangrove area and fishery yield are linked.
    • Biodiversity. The intertidal zone supports assemblages found nowhere else in the seascape.
    • Protection. Wave attenuation and sediment stabilisation reduce the energy reaching the shoreline.

    A policy that values only one of these will undervalue the system — which is exactly the problem Lesson 6.4 takes up as natural capital accounting.

    Lesson 5.4 — The nitrogen cycle

    Nitrogen is the opposite problem to carbon. The atmosphere is 78% N₂ — an enormous reservoir — but the triple bond makes it inert and unavailable to almost all organisms. Life therefore depends on a small number of microbial transformations that convert nitrogen between forms, and on the single industrial process that now rivals them in magnitude.

    Biological nitrogen fixation

    Fixation converts N₂ into biologically available ammonia, catalysed by the enzyme nitrogenase. The enzyme is irreversibly damaged by oxygen, so every fixing organism must solve the same problem — legume root nodules supply leghaemoglobin to buffer oxygen, many cyanobacteria confine fixation to specialised heterocysts, and others fix only at night or in anoxic microsites. Fixation is also energetically expensive, which is why it is favoured precisely where nitrogen is scarce and disfavoured where it is abundant.

    Symbiotic

    Rhizobium and relatives in legume nodules; Frankia in actinorhizal plants. The plant supplies carbon, the bacterium supplies nitrogen.

    Cyanobacteria

    Free-living and symbiotic, including the Azolla association used in rice systems. A major nitrogen input to many aquatic and paddy systems.

    Free-living

    Heterotrophic fixers in soils and sediments. Individually modest, collectively significant where carbon is abundant and nitrogen is scarce.

    The Haber–Bosch process

    Industrial fixation combines atmospheric N₂ with hydrogen under high temperature and pressure to make ammonia. Its consequences are genuinely double-sided, and the honest analysis is a trade-off rather than a verdict.

    What it enabled

    • Synthetic fertiliser at scale, and the yield increases of the twentieth century
    • Food production supporting a large share of the current human population
    • Reduced pressure to convert additional land where yields rose instead

    What it set in motion

    • Reactive nitrogen created on a scale comparable to all natural terrestrial fixation
    • Nitrate leaching, eutrophication and coastal hypoxia downstream
    • N₂O emissions — a potent greenhouse gas — and NH₃/NOₓ affecting air quality
    • Substantial energy demand, historically met largely from fossil feedstocks

    The nitrogen cascade

    Here is what makes nitrogen different from most pollutants. A single atom of reactive nitrogen is not consumed when it causes an effect. It moves on, changes form, and causes another — sequentially, in different places, through different mechanisms, until it is finally returned to N₂ by denitrification or anammox. Galloway and colleagues named this the nitrogen cascade, and it is the reason nitrogen policy cannot be organised one medium at a time.

    Lesson 5.5 — The phosphorus cycle

    Phosphorus has no significant gaseous phase in the classical cycle. That one structural difference changes everything about how it behaves, how it limits production, and how it is managed.

    Comparison of carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycles
    FeatureCarbonNitrogenPhosphorus

    Phosphorus commonly limits production in freshwater systems and in many highly weathered tropical soils, where it is bound tightly to iron and aluminium oxides. Because the supply is geological, finite and concentrated in a few countries, phosphorus is simultaneously an ecological problem (too much, downstream) and a resource-security problem (too little, eventually).

    Lesson 5.6 — The sulfur cycle

    Sulfur moves between oxidised and reduced forms through both geological and biological routes, and its chemistry connects acid deposition, wetland biogeochemistry and even cloud formation.

    • Geological and volcanic. Weathering of sulfide minerals and volcanic emission of SO₂ and H₂S supply sulfur to surface systems.
    • Sulfate (SO₄²⁻). The dominant oxidised form in oxic waters and soils; taken up by plants and microbes and incorporated into amino acids.
    • Microbial reduction. Under anoxia, sulfate-reducing bacteria use SO₄²⁻ as an electron acceptor, producing H₂S — the source of the characteristic smell of anoxic sediments and the agent that precipitates metal sulfides.
    • Atmospheric sulfur. Combustion-derived SO₂ oxidises to sulfate aerosol and returns as acid deposition; marine dimethyl sulfide is a natural source of sulfate aerosol with a role in cloud condensation nuclei.
    Think like an ecosystem ecologist

    Sulfate reduction in wetlands produces sulfide, which binds iron. In iron-rich sediments, iron normally binds phosphate and keeps it out of the water column. Predict what happens to phosphorus availability when sulfate loading to a wetland increases.

    Reasoning: sulfide competes for iron, forming FeS; the iron is then unavailable to bind phosphate, which is released to the water. A sulfur input has produced a phosphorus problem. This is coupling, and it is why single-element management repeatedly disappoints.

    Lesson 5.7 — The water cycle as a biogeochemical conveyor

    Evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, groundwater flow and runoff are familiar from Chapter 2. The addition here is chemical.

    Water is not merely another biogeochemical cycle — it is also the main transport mechanism for nutrients and dissolved substances.

    Almost every lateral transfer in this chapter is water-borne: nitrate leaching from a field, phosphorus bound to eroding sediment, dissolved organic carbon from peat, metals mobilised from a mine tailing. Water also controls the redox state of soils by determining oxygen supply, which in turn controls the chemical form of iron, manganese, sulfur, nitrogen and phosphorus. Hydrology is therefore not a separate chapter from biogeochemistry; it is the delivery system.

    Lesson 5.8 — Trace metals and redox control

    Many metals are essential micronutrients at low concentrations and harmful at high ones. The distinction is dose and chemical form, not identity.

    Essential micronutrients

    Iron, manganese, zinc, copper, molybdenum, nickel. Required in enzymes — nitrogenase needs iron and molybdenum; photosynthetic electron transport needs iron and copper. Iron limitation constrains primary production across large regions of the open ocean.

    Primarily toxic exposures

    Lead, mercury, cadmium, arsenic. No established biological requirement in humans, and toxicity depends sharply on speciation — methylmercury behaves very differently from inorganic mercury, and arsenite differently from arsenate.

    Metals move through weathering, biological uptake, adsorption onto mineral and organic surfaces, precipitation, sedimentation and remobilisation. Which of these dominates is decided largely by redox conditions and pH.

    Why this matters for management

    Flooding a soil moves it down this ladder within days. That is why a reservoir drawdown can release iron, manganese and phosphate into drinking water; why submerged rice paddies emit methane; and why dredging anoxic sediment can remobilise metals that had been locked up as sulfides for decades. Redox is the master variable, and water is its control.

    Week 6

    Ecosystem structure and function

    If sunlight delivers enormous amounts of energy to Earth, why can ecosystems support only a limited number of trophic levels?

    Because energy does not cycle. Every transfer loses most of what entered it, mainly as respiration and heat, and a quantity that is cut by roughly an order of magnitude at each step runs out quickly. Week 5 followed matter around a loop; Week 6 follows energy down a staircase, and then asks how any of it is measured.

    Lesson 6.1 — Energy flow and trophic structure

    Matter CYCLES

    A nitrogen atom can pass through soil, root, leaf, herbivore, detritus and back to soil indefinitely. Nothing is lost from the system except by export. Conservation of mass applies, so budgets close — the entire logic of Week 5.

    Energy FLOWS

    A photon's energy is captured once, passed along a few times with large losses, and finally dissipated as heat. It cannot be recaptured. The system needs a continuous external supply, which is why the sun sits outside every ecosystem diagram.

    The distinction that everything else depends on

    "Energy cycles through the ecosystem" is the most common and most consequential error in this field. If energy cycled, trophic levels would be unlimited and decomposers would return usable energy to producers. Neither is true. Decomposers return nutrients, not energy.

    Trophic roles

    Producers fix energy. Primary consumers eat producers. Secondary and tertiary consumers eat consumers. Detritivores physically fragment dead material; decomposers — mainly bacteria and fungi — mineralise it chemically. The last group is not a terminal step but a parallel channel: in many ecosystems the majority of NPP is never eaten alive and flows directly into the detrital pathway.

    Ecological efficiency

    Trophic transfer efficiency is the fraction of energy at one level that becomes production at the next. The familiar 10% figure is a convenient starting point and no more.

    Be precise about the "10% rule"

    It is a simplified teaching approximation. Measured trophic transfer efficiencies vary substantially — commonly from a few percent in terrestrial systems with woody, poorly digestible producers to appreciably higher values in open-water systems with small, nutritious, rapidly growing phytoplankton. Efficiency depends on the organisms, the tissue chemistry, the thermal environment and the pathway. Use 10% to build intuition; never use it to defend a number.

    Efficiency calculator and food-web explorer

    Set the transfer efficiency and watch the pyramid rebuild; then open the food web, highlight a species' connections, and remove one to trace direct and indirect effects.

    Real ecosystems are not linear chains. A food web has multiple pathways, omnivory, and species that occupy different positions at different life stages. That architecture matters for stability: a web with many weak links behaves very differently from a chain with a few strong ones, and the loss of one species can propagate through direct effects on its prey and predators and indirect effects on species it never touches — including trophic cascades, where a change at the top alters producer biomass at the bottom.

    Lesson 6.2 — Controls on primary production

    Production is not set by a single factor, and the factor that limits it changes with place, season and history.

    Terrestrial systems

    • Light — interception depends on leaf area and canopy structure
    • Temperature — enzyme kinetics and growing-season length
    • Water — stomatal conductance couples carbon gain to water loss
    • Nitrogen and phosphorus supply
    • Vegetation type and stand age

    Aquatic systems

    • Light penetration — attenuates with depth, turbidity and colour
    • Nutrient supply — often N in marine, P in freshwater, Fe in parts of the open ocean
    • Temperature and stratification
    • Mixing depth relative to the euphotic zone
    • Grazing pressure

    Liebig's law of the minimum

    Production is constrained by the resource in shortest supply relative to demand, not by the total quantity of all resources. The classic image is a barrel whose staves differ in height: the water level is set by the shortest stave, and lengthening any other stave changes nothing.

    Reason it through before you continue

    If an ecosystem receives abundant nitrogen but remains phosphorus-limited, will adding more nitrogen necessarily increase productivity?

    No — not while phosphorus remains the shortest stave. Added nitrogen accumulates, leaches, or is lost as gas, and may do downstream damage without producing any local benefit. Two honest complications: adding nitrogen can shift the system toward co-limitation, where both elements constrain production together, and sustained enrichment can change which species dominate even when total production does not move. "No effect on productivity" is not the same as "no effect".

    Lesson 6.3 — Decomposition, nutrient release and stoichiometry

    Decomposition is the engine of nutrient recycling: without it, nutrients would accumulate in dead organic matter and production would halt within a few years. Its rate depends on the environment and on the chemistry of the material.

    Temperature

    Raises microbial metabolic rate — but only while moisture and substrate permit.

    Moisture

    Both too dry and waterlogged slow decomposition; the optimum is intermediate.

    Oxygen

    Anaerobic decomposition is far slower and yields different end products, including CH₄.

    Substrate quality

    High lignin and high C:N slow decay; soft, nitrogen-rich tissue decays fast.

    Bacteria dominate decomposition of labile, nitrogen-rich substrates in moist, near-neutral conditions. Fungi are the principal decomposers of lignified material and tolerate drier, more acidic conditions; their hyphae can translocate nutrients across space. Detritivores — earthworms, termites, amphipods, millipedes — fragment material, increasing surface area and inoculating it with microbes. Removing the detritivores from a system typically slows decomposition even though they perform little of the chemical work themselves.

    Mineralisation and immobilisation

    Decomposer microbes both release and consume inorganic nutrients, and which dominates depends on the stoichiometry of what they are eating.

    Mineralisation

    Organic nutrient → inorganic form (organic N → NH₄⁺). Occurs when the substrate carries more nutrient than the microbes need per unit carbon, so the surplus is released. Plants gain.

    Immobilisation

    Inorganic nutrient → microbial biomass. Occurs when the substrate is nutrient-poor relative to its carbon, so microbes draw additional inorganic nutrient from the soil solution. Plants temporarily lose.

    Ecological stoichiometry

    Organisms are not interchangeable bags of carbon. Each has a characteristic elemental composition, and mismatches between what a consumer needs and what its food provides govern growth, nutrient release and population dynamics. The central ratio is C : N : P.

    CompartmentTypical C:N characterConsequence
    Woody plant tissueVery high C relative to N — carbon-rich, nutrient-poorSlow decomposition; immobilisation on incorporation; poor food quality
    Fresh leaf litterIntermediateDecomposition rate varies with species and with lignin content
    Soil microbial biomassRelatively low C:N — nutrient-rich and tightly constrainedMicrobes must acquire nutrients from the soil when the substrate is poor
    Animal consumersLow and homeostatically regulatedExcess dietary carbon is respired or excreted; nutrient-poor food limits growth

    In marine systems the canonical reference is the Redfield ratio, C : N : P ≈ 106 : 16 : 1 in phytoplankton biomass and, strikingly, in the deep ocean's dissolved nutrients. It is a broad average that emerges from the coupling between organism composition and seawater chemistry — not an invariant constant. Real values vary with taxon, growth rate, light and nutrient status, and the deviations are themselves informative.

    Go deeper — using N:P to infer limitation, and why it can mislead

    Comparing the N:P supply ratio to the organism's requirement ratio gives a first guess at which nutrient limits growth: supply N:P well below the requirement suggests nitrogen limitation, well above suggests phosphorus limitation. The approach is genuinely useful and genuinely fallible. It assumes the requirement ratio is known and fixed; it ignores the possibility that light, iron, silica or grazing is the actual constraint; and it describes the ratio of supply, which is not the same as the ratio of availability once sorption, speciation and recycling are accounted for. Ratios generate hypotheses. Enrichment experiments test them.

    Lesson 6.4 — Ecosystem services and natural capital

    Ecosystem processes become ecosystem services when they produce something people value. The framing is useful because it makes ecological function visible to decision-makers who would otherwise count only the converted land. It is contested because the translation is incomplete.

    Provisioning

    Food, fresh water, timber, fibre, fuel, genetic resources.

    Regulating

    Climate regulation, flood and storm regulation, water purification, pollination, disease regulation, carbon storage.

    Cultural

    Recreation, identity, education, aesthetic and spiritual value.

    Supporting

    Nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production. Note that frameworks differ: some treat these as underlying functions rather than services, to avoid double counting.

    Natural capital is the stock of natural resources and ecological systems that yields these flows of benefit — forests, wetlands, reefs, fisheries, watersheds, soils. The stock-and-flow language is deliberate and should be familiar from Lesson 5.1: capital is the reservoir, services are the flux, and a management decision that liquidates the stock to raise the flux is borrowing, not income.

    Valuation approachHow it worksPrincipal limitation
    Market priceUses observed prices for traded outputs such as timber or fishOnly captures what markets trade; ignores regulating and cultural services entirely
    Replacement costPrices the engineered substitute — a treatment plant for a filtering wetlandAssumes the substitute is equivalent and would actually be built
    Avoided damage costValues protection by the losses prevented, e.g. mangrove storm attenuationRequires a defensible counterfactual, which is often the whole argument
    Willingness to payStated or revealed preference surveys and travel-cost methodsSensitive to question framing, income and information; hypothetical bias
    Ecosystem accountingBuilds physical stock and flow accounts alongside monetary onesData-hungry; still developing standard practice in most countries
    A question with no settled answer

    Should every ecosystem service be assigned a monetary value?

    For: decisions are already made in monetary terms, and an unpriced service is implicitly valued at zero — which is how wetlands lose to car parks. Numbers make trade-offs explicit and auditable, and they make the loser in a trade-off visible.

    Against: monetisation presumes substitutability, which is exactly the weak-sustainability assumption Chapter 1 questioned. Cultural and spiritual values resist the metric, and the act of pricing can crowd out non-market reasons for protection. Aggregate figures also conceal distribution — who receives the benefit and who bears the loss.

    A middle position widely taken in practice: quantify in physical units always, monetise where the method is defensible, and mark explicitly what has been left out rather than letting omission read as zero.

    Philippine ecosystem case studies

    Mangroves

    Blue carbon storage in anoxic sediment, nursery habitat supporting offshore fisheries, and wave attenuation. Historic losses to aquaculture conversion make rehabilitation a live policy question — and one where species and site matching, not just hectares planted, decides whether the carbon and fishery functions actually return.

    Coral reefs

    High productivity and biodiversity in nutrient-poor water, sustained by tight internal recycling. Supports fisheries and tourism; threatened by warming, sedimentation from upstream land use, and nutrient enrichment that shifts competitive balance toward algae.

    Forested watersheds

    Couple hydrology to biogeochemistry: infiltration and sediment control upstream determine water supply, reservoir sedimentation and nutrient delivery downstream — a direct link back to the hydrograph work in Chapter 2.

    Agricultural landscapes and Laguna de Bay

    Fertiliser nitrogen and phosphorus, soil carbon management and productivity interact across the catchment; the lake, the country's largest, integrates upstream loading from agriculture, aquaculture, settlement and industry. It is a working illustration of the nitrogen cascade and of why management must be organised by catchment rather than by municipality.

    Lesson 6.5 — Measuring ecosystem function

    Ecosystem processes cannot be understood from theory alone. Scientists must measure exchanges of carbon, water, energy and nutrients.

    Eddy covariance

    The problem: you want the net exchange of CO₂ between a whole ecosystem and the atmosphere, continuously, without disturbing it. Chambers are too small and too intrusive. The solution exploits turbulence. Air above a canopy moves in eddies; at any instant some parcels travel upward and some downward, each carrying a CO₂ concentration. If upward-moving parcels are consistently poorer in CO₂ than downward-moving ones, the ecosystem is absorbing carbon.

    An instrument measuring vertical wind velocity and CO₂ concentration fast enough — typically ten or more times per second — can therefore compute the net flux as the covariance of the two fluctuating signals. The method integrates over a footprint of hundreds of metres upwind, which is why it is the standard for ecosystem-scale carbon exchange.

    Go deeper — the eddy covariance expression and its assumptions

    The vertical flux is proportional to the mean covariance of fluctuations in vertical wind velocity (w′) and the scalar of interest (c′), written compactly as F ≈ ρ · w′c′ averaged over the period, where ρ is air density and the primes denote deviations from the mean. Deriving it requires Reynolds decomposition and a set of assumptions worth knowing: horizontally homogeneous terrain, adequate turbulence, a steady state over the averaging interval, and a footprint that lies within the ecosystem of interest.

    Those assumptions fail most often at night, when turbulence collapses under stable stratification and respired CO₂ drains downslope instead of mixing upward. The resulting underestimate of night-time respiration is a known bias, usually addressed with a friction-velocity filter and gap-filling — procedures that are standard, defensible, and a reminder that a flux dataset is a processed product, not a raw observation.

    Long-term ecological research

    Ecosystems vary from year to year for reasons that have nothing to do with any trend: a strong El Niño, a typhoon, a mast seeding year, an insect outbreak. A two-year study cannot distinguish that variability from directional change, and it cannot observe succession, slow soil processes, or responses that only appear after a threshold is crossed. Long-term observation is not a luxury added to experiments; for a large class of questions it is the only instrument available.

    Explain it

    Why can adding nutrients ultimately reduce aquatic biodiversity?

    Enrichment raises production, but production is not the same as habitat quality. Algal growth shades submerged plants; the algal biomass eventually dies and sinks; decomposition of that organic matter consumes oxygen in bottom waters; and stratification prevents resupply from the surface. Oxygen-sensitive species are excluded or die, leaving a community dominated by a few tolerant taxa.

    Two refinements worth having: harmful algal blooms add direct toxicity to the oxygen mechanism, and the loss of large submerged plants removes structural habitat for fish and invertebrates — so biodiversity falls through at least three separate pathways, not one.

    The six questions to ask of any graph

    1 · What pattern is visible?

    Describe shape before cause: monotonic, seasonal, threshold, scattered.

    2 · What increases?

    Name the variable and the range over which it rises.

    3 · What decreases?

    And does it decrease everywhere, or only beyond a point?

    4 · Is there a relationship?

    Covariation is a pattern in the data, not yet a claim about the world.

    5 · Does it establish causation?

    Almost never. Observational covariation is consistent with reverse causation and with a shared driver.

    6 · What else would you need?

    An experiment, a control site, a longer record, a mechanism, or a measurement of the suspected confounder.

    Applications

    Case studies

    Four worked situations. Attempt each before opening the analysis — the reasoning is the assessable part, not the arithmetic.

    Natural and human-accelerated processes

    Humans rarely invent a biogeochemical process. What changes is the magnitude of a flux, its timing, its spatial distribution, and sometimes the chemical form in which an element is delivered. Each of those four changes has a different ecological consequence, and conflating them produces bad policy.

    ElementNatural processHuman-accelerated counterpartWhat actually changed
    CarbonSlow exchange with geological reservoirs via weathering, burial and volcanismFossil-fuel combustion; land clearingMagnitude and timing — slow-loop carbon injected into the fast loop in centuries rather than millions of years
    NitrogenBiological fixation by nitrogenase, concentrated where N is scarceHaber–Bosch fertiliser; combustion NOₓMagnitude and distribution — reactive N created at industrial scale and applied where it is already abundant
    PhosphorusRock weathering, in place, at geological ratesMining and global redistribution of phosphate rockSpatial pattern — P moved from a few deposits to croplands and then to water, with no return path
    SulfurVolcanism, sea spray, marine dimethyl sulfideFossil-fuel SO₂; subsequent emission controlsMagnitude in both directions — a large increase followed, in many regions, by a deliberate large decrease
    WaterPrecipitation, infiltration, evapotranspiration, runoffLand-use change, abstraction, impoundment, drainageTiming and routing — the same water delivered faster, or stored where it was not
    Keep the framing scientific

    Not every human modification is harmful, and saying so is not advocacy. Sulfur controls repaired acid deposition over large regions. Fertiliser raised yields and spared land. The analytical task is to identify which flux changed, by how much, where, and what followed — then to evaluate the trade-off explicitly rather than assume its sign.

    Case 1 · Forest carbon budget

    Scenario. A measurement campaign in a lowland forest reports the following for one year. Hypothetical instructional dataset

    QuantityValueUnits
    GPP3100g C m⁻² yr⁻¹
    Autotrophic respiration (Ra)1750g C m⁻² yr⁻¹
    Heterotrophic respiration (Rh)1180g C m⁻² yr⁻¹
    Biomass carbon stock18 400g C m⁻²
    Soil carbon stock (to 1 m)11 900g C m⁻²

    Compute NPP, ecosystem respiration and NEP, then decide whether the forest accumulated or lost carbon during the measurement year.

    NPP = GPP − Ra = 3100 − 1750 = 1350 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹.
    Re = Ra + Rh = 1750 + 1180 = 2930 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹.
    NEP = GPP − Re = 3100 − 2930 = +170 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ — a net sink for that year.

    Carbon-use efficiency = NPP/GPP = 1350/3100 ≈ 0.44, within the range commonly reported for forests.

    Now the interpretation that earns the marks. The stocks total 30 300 g C m⁻², so this year's NEP is roughly 0.6% of the standing stock. Three consequences follow. First, detecting that signal requires either flux measurement or decades of repeated inventory — a single biomass re-measurement cannot resolve it. Second, one year is not a trend: a drought year or a typhoon could reverse the sign without any change in the forest's long-run behaviour. Third, the budget ignores lateral export of dissolved and particulate carbon to streams and any disturbance loss, so the true net biome production is somewhat lower than +170.

    Case 2 · A lake through one season

    Scenario. Monthly monitoring of a shallow tropical lake receiving agricultural and settlement runoff. Hypothetical instructional dataset

    MonthTotal P (µg L⁻¹)Chlorophyll-a (µg L⁻¹)Bottom dissolved O₂ (mg L⁻¹)
    January2267.4
    February2897.0
    March41176.1
    April68344.3

    June112711.4

    Explain the chain connecting the three columns, and say what you would measure next to test it.

    The chain. Phosphorus loading rises through the wet months; chlorophyll-a — a proxy for algal biomass — tracks it with a lag of roughly a month; bottom oxygen falls as that biomass senesces, sinks and is decomposed by heterotrophs consuming oxygen faster than stratification allows it to be resupplied. By June the bottom water is below the 2 mg L⁻¹ hypoxia convention, and oxygen-sensitive benthic organisms are excluded.

    Note the lag, and the August values. Chlorophyll peaks a month after phosphorus and recovery begins before phosphorus has returned to January levels. Biological responses are not instantaneous, and internal loading from sediment — accelerated by the very anoxia the bloom created — can sustain the bloom after external inputs decline. That is a reinforcing feedback.

    What to measure next. Nitrogen as well as phosphorus, to establish which is limiting rather than assuming P. A vertical oxygen and temperature profile, to confirm stratification is preventing resupply. Sediment phosphorus flux, to quantify internal loading. And an upstream load estimate, to separate catchment inputs from in-lake recycling — the two require entirely different management responses.

    Case 3 · Food-web disturbance

    Scenario. A coastal system with the structure phytoplankton → zooplankton → small pelagic fish → large predatory fish. Fishing pressure causes a substantial decline in the large predators.

    Predict the possible consequences, and state clearly what makes the prediction uncertain.

    The textbook cascade. Releasing small pelagic fish from predation allows their population to rise; heavier grazing on zooplankton reduces zooplankton abundance; reduced grazing pressure on phytoplankton allows phytoplankton biomass to increase. Effects alternate in sign down the chain — the signature of a trophic cascade.

    Why the real outcome may differ. Marine food webs are webs, not chains. Small pelagics may be food-limited rather than predator-limited, in which case release produces little increase. Another predator may expand into the vacated role. Omnivory means some species feed at several levels, damping alternation. Zooplankton communities can shift in composition rather than abundance, changing grazing pressure in ways biomass does not reveal. And nutrient supply may constrain phytoplankton regardless of grazing — a bottom-up control overriding the top-down signal.

    The assessable judgement. A good answer names the mechanism, gives the expected direction, and then states the conditions under which it would fail. Treating the cascade as a guaranteed outcome is the error; so is refusing to predict at all.

    Case 4 · Drought and ecosystem carbon

    Scenario. A forest experiences an unusually prolonged dry season. Predict the direction of change in stomatal conductance, photosynthesis, GPP, respiration, NPP, soil decomposition, fire risk and NEP — then say what the net carbon effect depends on.

    The first-order chain. Soil water deficit → stomata close to limit water loss → CO₂ supply to the chloroplast falls → photosynthesis and therefore GPP decline → NPP declines. Meanwhile soil drying slows microbial activity, so heterotrophic respiration also declines. Fire risk rises as fuel moisture falls.

    Why NEP is not predictable from that alone. NEP is the difference between two quantities that are both falling. Whether it rises or falls depends on which falls faster — and that depends on drought severity, on how deeply rooted the vegetation is, and on soil texture and depth. A moderate drought can reduce respiration more than photosynthesis in some systems, briefly increasing NEP; a severe one reduces GPP sharply while respiration continues from stored substrate, pushing NEP negative.

    Duration changes the mechanism. Short drought: stomatal limitation, largely reversible. Prolonged drought: leaf shedding, hydraulic failure, mortality — which converts living biomass into a decomposing pool and can turn the stand into a multi-year carbon source long after the rain returns. Add fire and the loss becomes immediate and large.

    The systems point. One climatic perturbation has moved the water cycle, the carbon cycle, the nitrogen cycle (through reduced mineralisation and altered uptake) and the disturbance regime simultaneously. This is the integration the final challenge asks you to perform.

    Final synthesis — the ecosystem systems challenge

    Scenario. A tropical coastal watershed undergoes five simultaneous changes over two decades: forest clearing in the uplands, fertiliser intensification in the lowlands, warmer mean temperatures, increased rainfall variability, and mangrove decline at the coast.

    Predict the consequences for each of the nine components below. Write your reasoning in the box — it stays in your browser and is never transmitted — then compare it against the systems analysis.

    Carbon
    Nitrogen
    Phosphorus
    Water
    Primary production
    Decomposition
    Coastal oxygen
    Food-web structure
    Ecosystem services

    Carbon

    Clearing transfers biomass carbon to the atmosphere and exposes soil carbon to oxidation. Warming raises decomposition where moisture allows. Mangrove loss drains and oxidises centuries of sediment carbon. Net: a strong source, concentrated in the first years after disturbance.

    Nitrogen

    Fertiliser raises reactive N inputs; clearing removes the vegetation that intercepted it; rainfall variability delivers it in pulses. Expect higher nitrate leaching, higher N₂O, and the full cascade downstream.

    Phosphorus

    Delivered mainly with eroding sediment, so clearing matters more than fertiliser rate alone. Pulsed rainfall moves most of the annual load in a few storms — which is why mean annual concentrations understate the problem.

    Water

    Reduced infiltration and faster routing: higher, earlier flood peaks and lower dry-season baseflow. Variability amplifies both tails. This is the Chapter 2 hydrograph result reappearing as a biogeochemical driver.

    Primary production

    Falls on cleared uplands; rises sharply in the receiving waters as nutrients arrive. Production has not been lost from the system — it has been relocated to where it does harm.

    Decomposition

    Faster where warm and moist, suppressed in dry spells, and shifted toward anaerobic pathways in waterlogged and eutrophic sediments — which changes the products from CO₂ toward CH₄.

    Coastal oxygen

    Falls. Nutrient-driven production sinks and decomposes; warmer water holds less oxygen and stratifies more strongly; and the mangroves that previously intercepted some nutrient load are gone. Three mechanisms, one direction.

    Food-web structure

    Simplification. Hypoxia excludes sensitive benthic taxa; nursery habitat loss reduces recruitment of species harvested offshore; the community shifts toward tolerant, fast-turnover taxa with shorter chains.

    Ecosystem services

    Provisioning down (fisheries, water quality), regulating down (flood attenuation, storm protection, carbon storage), cultural down. Agricultural provisioning rises — which is the actual trade-off being made, and it should be stated in those terms rather than hidden.

    What the question was really testing

    Not nine separate predictions, but the couplings: that clearing changes hydrology, which changes nutrient delivery timing, which changes coastal production, which changes oxygen, which changes the food web, which changes the services people receive. A list of nine independent answers scores far below an analysis that traces the connections — because managing any one component in isolation will fail for exactly that reason.

    Consolidation

    Summary

    Seven messages, four comparison tables, one systems map, one concept map. If you can reconstruct these from memory, you have the chapter.

    Key messages

    Matter cycles

    Elements move among biological, atmospheric, aquatic, soil, sedimentary and geological reservoirs, and the same atom can make the circuit indefinitely.

    Energy flows

    Energy enters as radiation, passes through a few trophic transfers losing most of its magnitude at each, and dissipates as heat. It is never recycled, which is why food chains are short.

    Fluxes connect reservoirs

    Understanding ecosystem function means measuring rates, not just pool sizes. A large stock does not imply a large current flux — and only fluxes respond to management.

    Carbon connects ecosystems and climate

    Photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, ocean exchange, soils and sediments jointly regulate atmospheric CO₂ — and the atmosphere is the smallest of the fast reservoirs.

    N and P regulate productivity

    Both cycles have been substantially altered by human activity — industrial fixation for nitrogen, mining and redistribution for phosphorus — with eutrophication as the shared downstream consequence.

    Processes are interconnected

    A change in climate, nutrients, hydrology, organisms or land use propagates. Sulfate loading becomes a phosphorus problem; forest clearing becomes a coastal oxygen problem.

    Measurement matters

    Flux towers, long-term observation, manipulative experiments, isotopes and models each answer a different question, and none of them answers all of them.
