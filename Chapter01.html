Week 1 Foundations and systems thinking

1.1 What environmental science is, and what its method can deliver

Environmental science studies how the physical, chemical and biological components of the earth interact, how human activity perturbs those interactions, and what follows from the perturbation. It borrows the methods of chemistry, physics, ecology, geology and hydrology, and it borrows the questions of economics, law, politics and ethics. That breadth is not a weakness to be apologised for. It is a consequence of the object of study: an environmental problem that could be resolved inside a single discipline

would already have been.

The harder point concerns method. Classical experimental science isolates a variable, holds everything else constant, replicates, and randomises. Environmental systems refuse almost all of that. They are open — energy and matter cross their boundaries continuously. They are large, so manipulation is expensive and often unethical. They are frequently unique, so there is no replicate Laguna de Bay to serve as a control. And they operate on timescales that outrun a funded project.

The field therefore substitutes a portfolio of weaker inferential tools and compensates by triangulating among them:

  • Natural experiments — a volcanic eruption, a policy that applies on one side of a border, an accidental release.
  • Space-for-time substitution — comparing sites along a gradient of the variable of interest, accepting that the sites differ in other ways too.
  • Long-term monitoring — the only way to detect slow trends, and the first thing cut when budgets contract.
  • Process models — encoding mechanism explicitly so that assumptions become inspectable rather than implicit.
  • Mesocosm and microcosm experiments — genuine control, purchased at the cost of realism.
The characteristic failure

Because environmental data are usually observational, confounding — not sample size, not instrument precision — is the dominant threat to a causal claim. Nitrate in a river correlates with fertiliser sales; it also correlates with urbanisation, rainfall, land conversion and septic density, all of which moved together over the same decades. The remedy is not a larger dataset. It is a design or a model that can separate the candidate causes.

1.2 How the field got its assumptions: four paradigms

Current environmental practice carries the fossils of four successive framings. Recognising which one a proposal is speaking from usually explains an otherwise baffling disagreement.

ParadigmRoughlyCentral claim, and what it licenses
Utilitarian conservation1890s–1940sNature is a stock of resources to be managed for the greatest good of the greatest number over the longest time. Licenses scientific forestry, sustained yield, multiple use — and extraction, provided it is regulated.
Preservation1890s–Some places have value that use would destroy, so they should be kept from use altogether. Licenses strict reserves and wilderness designation.
Modern environmentalism1960s–1980sPollution and technology impose diffuse, invisible, involuntary risks on the public. Licenses regulation, standards, environmental impact assessment, the right to know.
Sustainability science1987–Environment, economy and equity are one coupled system operating across generations. Licenses integrated assessment, the SDGs, and the language of trade-offs rather than prohibition.
Definition — sustainable development

Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Commission, 1987). Note what the definition does not do: it does not say who defines a need, how competing present needs are ranked against future ones, or how a trade-off is adjudicated when it is genuinely a trade-off. Its usefulness and its emptiness have the same source.

Weak and strong sustainability

The distinction matters more than any other in this chapter, because it decides whether a mitigation offer is acceptable. Weak sustainability holds that natural capital and manufactured capital are substitutable: destroying a mangrove is acceptable if the value it produced is replaced by an engineered structure and a compensation fund, so long as total capital does not decline. Strong sustainability holds that some natural capital is critical — non-substitutable, and often irreversible once lost — so it must be maintained in physical terms regardless of compensating value elsewhere.

Planetary boundaries

The planetary boundaries framework (Rockström and colleagues, 2009; updated since) proposes a set of biophysical processes with quantitative limits inside which humanity has a safe operating space resembling Holocene conditions. The boundaries include climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change, biogeochemical flows of nitrogen and phosphorus, ocean acidification, stratospheric ozone depletion, atmospheric aerosol loading, and novel entities.

Two properties are constantly misread. First, a boundary is placed deliberately below the estimated threshold, in the lower part of the uncertainty range — crossing it signals entry into a zone of increasing risk, not the arrival of catastrophe. Second, several boundaries are global aggregates of regional processes. The nitrogen boundary is transgressed globally while large regions remain nitrogen-deficient, because fertiliser use is spatially concentrated. A global index can be exceeded and locally irrelevant at the same time.

1.3 Systems thinking: stocks, flows, feedback and residence time

Almost every quantitative argument in this course reduces to bookkeeping on a defined system. Draw a boundary, count what is inside it, count what crosses it, and account for the difference.

dM/dt = ΣFin − ΣFout The mass balance equation. At steady state dM/dt = 0, the stock is constant and inflow equals outflow. Steady state says nothing about whether the stock is large, small, healthy or degraded — only that it is not changing.
τ = M / F Residence time: the average time a unit of material spends in the reservoir. Equivalently, the time constant of the reservoir's response to a change in loading.
Worked example 1 — residence time and recovery

A lake holds 2.0 × 107 m³ and has inflow equal to outflow at 5.0 × 104 m³ d−1.

Step 1. τ = M/F = (2.0 × 107) / (5.0 × 104) = 400 days.

Step 2. Suppose a conservative contaminant has been entering at a constant rate and the source is abruptly eliminated. If the lake is well mixed and the only loss is flushing, the concentration decays exponentially, C(t) = C₀e−t/τ. One residence time removes about 63%, two about 86%, three about 95%.

Step 3. So roughly 3τ ≈ 1,200 days, a little over three years, before the lake is 95% cleaned — provided nothing has partitioned into the sediment, in which case sediment becomes a secondary source and the recovery time is set by that slower reservoir instead.

Interpretation. The residence time is a policy quantity, not just a physical one. It tells a regulator how long after a successful intervention the public should be told to expect visible improvement.

Feedback

A feedback exists when a change in a stock alters a flux that in turn alters the same stock. Reinforcing (positive) feedbacks amplify a perturbation and destabilise; balancing (negative) feedbacks oppose it and stabilise. The signs are not value judgements — a reinforcing feedback can amplify a recovery just as readily as a collapse.

Common error

Students routinely label a feedback "positive" because its outcome is desirable, or "negative" because it is harmful. The sign refers only to whether the loop amplifies or damps the original change. Ice–albedo is a positive feedback that produces a bad outcome; CO₂ fertilisation is a negative feedback that partially protects us. Neither sign carries moral content.

Budget closure and where to spend measurement effort

A budget "closes" when measured inputs equal measured outputs plus the measured change in storage. Real budgets rarely close. A 30% residual does not mean mass was destroyed; it means a flux is unmeasured, or one of the measured fluxes carries a systematic bias. The residual is diagnostic information, and the standard next step is to ask which term, given its magnitude and its relative uncertainty, could plausibly account for it. That same reasoning tells you where to spend the next research peso: not on the largest flux, and not on the easiest to measure, but on whichever term has the largest product of magnitude and relative uncertainty.

1.4 Ethics, justice and knowledge systems

Every environmental decision contains a valuation that no measurement can supply. Three positions recur:

  • Anthropocentric — nature has instrumental value; it matters because and insofar as it matters to humans. This is the operating assumption of nearly all environmental economics and most environmental law.
  • Biocentric — individual living organisms have intrinsic value and moral standing, independent of human use.
  • Ecocentric — the locus of value is the whole system: populations, species, communities and the abiotic processes that sustain them. Granting a river legal personhood is an ecocentric move, because it treats an entity that is not an organism as a rights-bearer.
Definition — environmental justice, in three dimensions

Distributive: who receives the benefits, and who bears the burdens and risks. Procedural: who gets to participate in the decision, with what information, and with what actual power to change the outcome. Recognition: whose identity, culture and knowledge are acknowledged as legitimate in the first place. A project can satisfy one dimension and fail another — a consultation that is held, minuted and legally sufficient but that could never have altered the design is a procedural failure regardless of how the benefits are ultimately distributed.

Studies in several countries have found that hazardous facilities are sited disproportionately in minority communities even after controlling for income and land value. That residual is why the literature uses the term environmental racism rather than treating siting purely as a market outcome: price alone does not explain the pattern, so something about political power and recognition is doing work.

Indigenous and local knowledge (ILK) is a knowledge system with its own methods of observation, transmission and validation, frequently containing long-term ecological information no monitoring programme possesses — historical fire regimes, species distributions before disturbance, seasonal timing. The productive stance is co-production: ILK and scientific knowledge treated as complementary systems, negotiated under free, prior and informed consent, rather than ILK being mined for data points and then re-validated against Western metrics before it is allowed to count.

Week 2 Drivers, data and the policy frame

1.5 What drives environmental change

Impact is not caused by population alone, or by consumption alone. The oldest decomposition makes this explicit.

I = P × A × T Impact = Population × Affluence (consumption per person) × Technology (impact per unit of consumption). A multiplicative identity, so a proportional change in any term changes impact proportionally — and improvements in T can be entirely erased by growth in P and A.
Worked example 2 — why efficiency gains disappear

Over a decade a country's population grows 20%, consumption per person grows 50%, and impact per unit of consumption falls 40%.

Inew/Iold = (1.20)(1.50)(0.60) = 1.08

A 40% technological improvement — a substantial industrial achievement — yields an 8% increase in total impact. This is the arithmetic behind the persistent gap between efficiency headlines and emissions inventories.

The Kaya identity is the same logic specialised to carbon:

CO₂ = Population × (GDP/Population) × (Energy/GDP) × (CO₂/Energy) Population, income per capita, energy intensity of the economy, and carbon intensity of energy. For emissions to fall while GDP grows at 3% per year, energy intensity and carbon intensity must together improve by more than 3% per year, sustained.

Two further concepts complete the picture. The ecological footprint converts consumption into the biologically productive area required to supply it and absorb its carbon dioxide, and compares that to available biocapacity. A country whose footprint exceeds its biocapacity runs an ecological deficit, which it meets by importing biocapacity, drawing down its own stocks, or using the global atmosphere as a sink. Its usefulness is communicative; its weakness is that it compresses toxicity, biodiversity loss and water scarcity into a single land-area unit dominated by the carbon component.

The rebound effect explains why the T term underdelivers. When efficiency lowers the cost of an energy service, consumption of that service typically rises, recovering part of the saving; where the effect exceeds 100% — historically argued for some general-purpose technologies — it is called the Jevons paradox. Lighting is the standard illustration: efficacy has improved by orders of magnitude while total lighting energy has not fallen correspondingly, because cheap light gets used more lavishly.

1.6 The triple planetary crisis and its couplings

The UN framing groups the global environmental agenda into climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution and waste. The framing is useful only if the couplings are kept in view, because policies aimed at one can worsen another:

  • Land converted for bioenergy reduces emissions per unit energy while removing habitat and often increasing nutrient loading.
  • Sulfate aerosol pollution cools the surface; cleaning the air unmasks warming that was always present in the forcing.
  • Warming shifts species ranges into landscapes fragmented by land-use change, so climate and biodiversity losses compound rather than add.
  • Nitrogen fertiliser raises yields, sparing land from conversion, while degrading downstream water quality and emitting nitrous oxide.

Recognising a coupling is what distinguishes an integrated assessment from a sectoral one, and it is the reason single-issue optimisation is treated with suspicion in this course.

1.7 Environmental data: what a number is worth

Environmental measurements arrive with structure that determines what may legitimately be done with them.

ConceptWhat it means, and the mistake it prevents
Accuracy vs precisionAccuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is repeatability. Three readings of 3.412, 3.415, 3.413 mg L−1 on a 5.00 mg L−1 standard are highly precise and badly inaccurate — a calibration bias, invisible to any measure of scatter.
Method detection limitThe lowest concentration reportable as different from zero. Values below it are censored, not zero. Substituting zero biases means low; substituting the detection limit biases them high; the defensible options are substitution at MDL/2 with the choice disclosed, or a statistical method for censored data.
Scale and supportA relationship observed at one spatial or temporal scale need not hold at another. Inferring individual exposure from area-level averages is the ecological fallacy.
Uncertainty vs variabilityVariability is real heterogeneity in the system and cannot be reduced by more measurement. Uncertainty is our ignorance and can. Conflating them produces either false precision or paralysis.
Indicators and indicesA composite index requires choosing variables, normalising them, and weighting them. The weights are value judgements wearing arithmetic clothing: two countries can score identically with opposite profiles, and re-weighting can reverse a ranking without any data changing.

1.8 The governance architecture

Environmental law operates at three nested levels, and a project is normally subject to all three at once.

International

Multilateral environmental agreements differ enormously in effectiveness, and the differences are structural rather than moral. The Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting substances is the standard success: a small number of producing firms, technically feasible substitutes that were already being developed, a clear and measurable set of controlled chemicals, differentiated timetables with a financial mechanism for developing countries, and trade restrictions against non-parties that made defection costly. Climate agreements confront the opposite of nearly every one of those conditions — fossil energy is diffuse through every sector, substitutes touch entire capital stocks, and there is no equivalent point of leverage. Comparing the two teaches design, not virtue.

Other instruments worth knowing by function: the CBD and its Kunming–Montreal framework for biodiversity, UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement for climate, Basel, Rotterdam and Stockholm for wastes and chemicals, Ramsar for wetlands, and the Sustainable Development Goals as a non-binding but agenda-setting frame, of which SDGs 6, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 15 are directly environmental.

National — the Philippine frame

The concurrency point

Statutes apply simultaneously, not in sequence. Securing an ECC under PD 1586 does not extinguish an obligation arising from a protected area's own designation, from IPRA's consent requirement, or from a water quality management area's discharge rules. A common examination trap — and a common real-world failure — is to treat one clearance as if it settled the others.

Key terms

Open systemA system exchanging matter and energy across its boundary; the normal case in environmental science and the reason controls are scarce.
Stock and fluxQuantity held in a reservoir, and the rate at which material crosses its boundary.
Residence time (τ)M/F at steady state; also the time constant of the reservoir's response to changed loading.
Steady stateInputs equal outputs so the stock is constant. Not a statement about the stock's condition.
Reinforcing feedbackLoop that amplifies a perturbation. Sign describes dynamics, not desirability.
Balancing feedbackLoop that opposes a perturbation and stabilises the stock.
Weak sustainabilityNatural and manufactured capital treated as substitutable; total capital must not decline.
Strong sustainabilityCritical natural capital must be maintained physically; substitution is not accepted.
Planetary boundaryA quantified limit set below an estimated threshold, marking entry into a zone of increasing risk.
IPAT / Kaya identityMultiplicative decompositions of impact and of carbon emissions into their proximate drivers.
Rebound effectPart of an efficiency saving recovered by increased use of the cheaper service.
Procedural justiceWhether affected people had real power to shape the decision, not merely a hearing.
Method detection limitLowest concentration distinguishable from zero; values below it are censored, not zero.
Ecological fallacyInferring individual-level relationships from group-level averages.
DPSIRDrivers–Pressures–State–Impacts–Responses; forces an indicator to declare what it measures.
Free, prior and informed consentConsent obtained before a decision, with full disclosure, and genuinely refusable.

What to take away

  • Environmental science works on open, unreplicated systems, so confounding — not sample size — is the standing threat to causal claims, and triangulation across weak designs substitutes for the controlled experiment.
  • Four paradigms still shape practice; identifying which one an argument is speaking from usually explains the disagreement faster than debating the evidence.
  • Mass balance and residence time convert vague concern into a number, and τ tells a decision-maker how long after a successful intervention improvement should become visible.
  • Feedback signs describe amplification or damping, never desirability.
  • IPAT and Kaya show why large efficiency gains routinely fail to reduce total impact, and the rebound effect explains part of the shortfall.
  • Every number carries a method, a detection limit, a scale and an uncertainty; every composite index carries a weighting that is a value judgement.
  • Environmental statutes apply concurrently. One clearance never settles the others.
