Week 3 The coupled earth system

2.1 Five subsystems, one system

The division of the earth into atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere and biosphere is an analytical convenience, not a physical partition. Every boundary between them is a site of exchange, and most of the phenomena this course treats as problems are exchanges that changed rate. Carbon moves from lithosphere to atmosphere when coal burns; water moves from cryosphere to hydrosphere when ice melts; nitrogen moves from atmosphere to biosphere through an industrial process that did not exist before 1913.

What distinguishes the subsystems operationally is not composition but response time. A perturbation propagates through the atmosphere in days, through the surface ocean in years, through the deep ocean in centuries, and through the lithosphere over geological time. This is the residence-time logic of Chapter 1 applied at planetary scale, and it explains why some consequences of a decision arrive during a political term and others do not arrive for a thousand years.

SubsystemPrincipal reservoirsCharacteristic response time
AtmosphereGases, aerosols, water vapourDays to weeks for mixing; a decade for well-mixed tropospheric gases to equilibrate globally
HydrosphereOceans, lakes, rivers, groundwaterDays for rivers, years for surface ocean, centuries to a millennium for the deep ocean, decades to millennia for aquifers
CryosphereSea ice, glaciers, ice sheets, permafrostSeasonal for sea ice; centuries to millennia for ice sheets — the source of most sea-level commitment
LithosphereCrust, mantle, soils, sedimentsMillions of years for tectonics; decades to millennia for soil formation, which is why soil is treated as non-renewable
BiosphereVegetation, soils organic matter, marine lifeDays for plankton, decades to centuries for forests, and effectively permanent for extinction

2.2 The energy balance, and the circulation it drives

Everything that moves in the atmosphere and ocean is ultimately driven by one fact: the earth receives more solar energy per unit area at the equator than at the poles, and the resulting imbalance must be transported poleward. Before that, the planetary budget itself.

Te = [ S(1 − α) / 4σ ]1/4 Planetary radiative equilibrium temperature. S ≈ 1361 W m⁻² is the solar constant, α ≈ 0.30 the planetary albedo, σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. The factor of 4 converts from the intercepting disc (πr²) to the emitting sphere (4πr²).
Worked example 1 — the 33 kelvin question

Step 1. S(1 − α)/4 = 1361 × 0.70 ÷ 4 = 238 W m⁻².

Step 2. Te = (238 ÷ 5.67 × 10⁻⁸)1/4 ≈ (4.20 × 109)1/4 ≈ 255 K, or −18 °C.

Step 3. Observed mean surface temperature is about 288 K. The 33 K difference is the natural greenhouse effect.

Interpretation. Now perturb it: raising albedo from 0.30 to 0.32 — plausible for a large volcanic aerosol loading — lowers Te by about 1.9 K. The same arithmetic shows why an ice-albedo feedback is powerful, and why solar geoengineering proposals are arithmetically cheap and institutionally terrifying.

Because the surplus at low latitudes must be exported, the atmosphere organises itself into circulation cells. Air rises where heating is greatest, moves poleward aloft, sinks where it has cooled and lost moisture, and returns equatorward at the surface. The consequence is a banded pattern of wet and dry climates that explains the position of the world's rainforests and its great deserts, and it is the single most useful climate diagram a graduate student can carry.

The ocean carries roughly comparable heat poleward by a different mechanism. Wind stress drives the surface gyres; density differences arising from temperature and salinity drive the deep thermohaline circulation, whose overturning timescale of order a millennium sets the deep ocean's response time in the table above. Because the two circulations exchange heat and gas at the surface, they cannot be modelled separately — the coupled atmosphere–ocean model is not a refinement but a necessity.

2.3 The solid earth: tectonics, rock cycle, weathering and soils

Plate tectonics supplies the slow boundary conditions for everything above. It determines where mountains rise and therefore where orographic rainfall and erosion concentrate; it opens and closes ocean gateways that reorganise circulation; it recycles carbon through subduction and volcanism on million-year timescales; and it places the Philippines where it is — on a convergent margin, which is simultaneously the reason for the archipelago's mineral wealth, its volcanic soils, its seismicity and its typhoon-exposed topography.

Weathering is where the rock cycle meets environmental chemistry. Physical weathering fragments rock and multiplies surface area; chemical weathering dissolves it. Silicate weathering consumes atmospheric carbon dioxide and is the long-term thermostat of the climate system, operating over hundreds of thousands of years — far too slowly to help with the present perturbation, which is precisely the point that makes enhanced weathering proposals interesting and contested.

Soil is the product, and it is the most consequential interface in the chapter: it stores more carbon than vegetation and atmosphere combined, it mediates every nutrient cycle in Chapter 3, and it controls the partitioning of rainfall between infiltration and runoff. Its formation is governed by climate, organisms, relief, parent material and time, and its structure is legible in a profile.

2.4 Water: the hydrologic cycle and the watershed

Water is the integrator. It links the atmosphere to the land surface, transports sediment and solutes, and defines the unit of analysis this course will use repeatedly.

Worked example 2 — atmospheric water residence time

The atmosphere holds roughly 12.7 × 10³ km³ of water. Global precipitation is roughly 496 × 10³ km³ per year, and at steady state evaporation matches it.

τ = M/F = 12.7 × 10³ ÷ 496 × 10³ = 0.0256 yr ≈ 9.3 days.

Interpretation. Atmospheric water turns over about forty times a year. This is why water vapour cannot be a climate forcing — any excess rains out within days, so vapour concentration is set by temperature rather than by emission, which makes it a fast feedback instead. Apply the same calculation to the deep ocean and the answer is over a thousand years.

Definition — watershed (catchment, drainage basin)

The area of land from which all surface runoff drains to a common outlet, bounded by the topographic divide. It is the natural accounting unit for water, sediment and nutrients, because the boundary is defined by the process itself rather than by administrative convenience. Note that the groundwater divide does not always coincide with the topographic one, which is how a catchment budget can leak.

The most policy-relevant behaviour of a catchment is how it converts a storm into a flow. Land cover governs the split between infiltration and overland flow, and urbanisation shifts that split decisively: impervious surfaces and engineered drainage deliver water to the channel faster, so the same rainfall produces a higher, earlier peak and a shorter lag.

Common error

Treating "more flooding" as evidence of "more rain". A catchment that has been paved, drained and channelised produces higher peaks from an unchanged climate. Attribution requires separating the rainfall signal from the land-cover signal — which is a spatial analysis problem, and therefore the bridge into Week 4.

Week 4 GIS, remote sensing and spatial analysis

2.5 The spatial workflow, end to end

A geographic information system is not a mapping program. It is a system for storing, querying and analysing data that carry location, and the analysis is the point — the map is only the last step, and frequently the least defensible one. Every project in this chapter follows the same chain, and most failures can be traced to a step that was skipped rather than to a tool that was misused.

2.6 Data models: vector and raster

Two ways of digitising the world, each with an implicit theory of what space is. The vector model treats space as occupied by discrete objects with sharp boundaries, stored as points, lines and polygons with attached attribute tables. The raster model treats space as a continuous surface sampled on a regular grid, where each cell carries one value. Choosing between them is a substantive modelling decision, not a file-format preference.

QuestionVector is better when…Raster is better when…
What is being representedDiscrete objects with legal or physical boundaries — parcels, roads, protected area limitsContinuous fields — elevation, temperature, reflectance, concentration
Typical operationTopological queries, network analysis, exact area and lengthMap algebra, terrain derivatives, image processing
Storage behaviourEfficient for sparse features; complex geometry costsSize scales with extent ÷ cell size squared — halving the cell quadruples the file
Characteristic weaknessImposes crisp lines on gradual transitions such as an ecotoneBoundary position quantised to the grid; mixed pixels at every edge

2.7 Georeferencing: datums, projections and PRS92

A coordinate is meaningless without the reference frame that defines it. Two layers can each be internally perfect and still disagree on the ground by hundreds of metres, and this is by far the most common source of wrong answers in student GIS work.

Definition — the three-part reference

An ellipsoid is the mathematical shape approximating the earth. A datum fixes that ellipsoid to the physical earth with a defined origin and orientation. A projection then flattens the curved surface to a plane. A full coordinate reference system specifies all three, which is what an EPSG code encodes in a single number.

The Philippine case

The national frame is the Philippine Reference System of 1992 (PRS92), maintained by NAMRIA and used for cadastral and most official mapping; geographic coordinates on PRS92 carry EPSG:4683, and the five national plane zones carry EPSG:3121 through 3125. Satellite data, GNSS handhelds and web basemaps deliver WGS84 coordinates instead, and UTM Zone 51N (EPSG:32651) covers most of the archipelago. These frames are not interchangeable: mixing an older local datum with WGS84 produces offsets large enough to move a parcel corner across a road, so transformation parameters must be obtained from the mapping agency rather than guessed. Always verify the current authoritative code and transformation in the EPSG registry before production work.

Worked example 3 — scale, resolution and what you may claim

A land-cover map is produced from 30 m Landsat imagery and printed at 1:50,000.

Step 1. At 1:50,000, one centimetre on the page equals 500 m on the ground, so a 30 m pixel occupies 0.6 mm — below the width of a printed line.

Step 2. A hectare is 10,000 m²; a 30 m pixel is 900 m². Roughly 11 pixels make a hectare, and 1,111 pixels make a square kilometre.

Step 3. A defensible minimum mapping unit is conventionally several pixels — say a 3 × 3 block, 0.81 ha — because single-pixel patches are dominated by mixed-pixel effects and misclassification.

Interpretation. You may not report "0.2 ha of mangrove lost" from this data. The claim exceeds the resolution, and the number will not survive a competent reviewer.

2.8 Acquisition: sensors, platforms and the resolution trade-off

Remote sensing measures electromagnetic radiation reflected or emitted by a surface. Because different materials interact with different wavelengths in characteristic ways, the spectrum itself carries the information — and the choice of bands determines what can be seen.

The resolution trade-off

Spatial, spectral, temporal and radiometric resolution compete for a fixed budget of photons and downlink bandwidth. A sensor with centimetre pixels cannot also image the whole country daily. The correct question is never "what is the highest resolution available" but "what is the smallest feature and the shortest interval my question actually requires" — and in the Philippines, cloud cover frequently makes revisit frequency, not pixel size, the binding constraint.

2.9 Analysis: band math, terrain, interpolation and overlay

Spectral indices

Terrain from a digital elevation model

Interpolation

Field measurements are points; the phenomena are surfaces. Interpolation bridges the gap, and the method encodes an assumption. Inverse distance weighting assumes only that near things are more alike, and produces characteristic bull's-eyes around each sample. Kriging first models the spatial autocorrelation structure explicitly through a variogram, then uses it to produce both an estimate and a mapped estimation variance — the second output being the reason it is preferred in environmental work, since it shows where the map is guessing.

Overlay and multi-criteria suitability

The operation that makes GIS an analytical tool rather than a drawing tool. Boolean overlay intersects binary constraints — slope under 18%, outside the protected area, more than 500 m from a watercourse — and returns the areas satisfying all of them. Weighted overlay instead standardises each criterion to a common scale, assigns weights reflecting relative importance, and sums. The weights are usually derived by pairwise comparison (AHP), which also yields a consistency ratio; a ratio above about 0.1 signals that the judgements are internally contradictory and should be revisited.

Worked example 4 — a suitability score

For one candidate cell, the standardised criterion scores are slope 0.8, land cover 0.6, distance to river 0.9, road access 0.4, with the weights shown above.

S = (0.40 × 0.8) + (0.30 × 0.6) + (0.20 × 0.9) + (0.10 × 0.4) = 0.32 + 0.18 + 0.18 + 0.04 = 0.72

Interpretation. Now test the result rather than reporting it. If slope and land cover were weighted equally at 0.35 each, the score becomes 0.72 as well — stable. But a cell scoring 0.9 on slope and 0.2 on land cover would move from 0.62 to 0.58 and might cross a decision threshold. That is what a sensitivity analysis is for, and it is the difference between a suitability map and a suitability argument.

2.10 Classification, accuracy and change detection

Turning imagery into a thematic map means assigning each pixel to a class. Unsupervised classification clusters the spectral data first and asks the analyst to label the clusters afterwards. Supervised classification starts from training samples of known classes and generalises from them, increasingly using machine-learning classifiers such as random forests. Either way, the output is a hypothesis about the ground, and it is worth nothing until tested against independent reference data.

Worked example 5 — reading the matrix

Overall accuracy = 169/200 = 84.5%. Producer's accuracy for crop = 48/60 = 80.0% — one fifth of real cropland was missed. User's accuracy for crop = 48/60 = 80.0% as well, here by coincidence of the totals.

Expected agreement by chance = Σ(row total × column total)/n² = [(70 × 75) + (60 × 60) + (70 × 65)] ÷ 200² = (5,250 + 3,600 + 4,550) ÷ 40,000 = 0.335.

Kappa = (0.845 − 0.335) ÷ (1 − 0.335) = 0.510 ÷ 0.665 ≈ 0.77.

Interpretation. Substantial agreement beyond chance, but the crop class is the weak one in both directions — so a conclusion resting on cropland area carries more error than the headline 84.5% suggests. Report the matrix, not just the percentage.

2.11 Cartography, metadata and spatial uncertainty

The final step is where an analysis becomes a claim that other people act on. Three obligations attach to it.

  • Design honestly. Classification breaks decide what the reader sees: equal interval, quantile and natural breaks applied to the same data produce maps that support different conclusions. Choose the scheme for a stated reason and say what it was. Use sequential colour ramps for ordered data and diverging ramps only where a meaningful midpoint exists, and avoid ramps that lose their ordering for colour-blind readers.
  • Document. Metadata records the source, acquisition date, datum and projection, processing steps, accuracy assessment and licence. Without it the layer cannot be reused or audited, and in a regulatory setting an undocumented layer is close to worthless.
  • Show what you do not know. Positional error, attribute error and temporal mismatch all propagate through an overlay. A suitability map built from four layers inherits the error of all four, and the honest map either carries an uncertainty companion or states the limitation in the legend.
Two traps worth naming

The modifiable areal unit problem: results change when the same data are aggregated into different zones or resolutions, so a relationship demonstrated at barangay level may vanish at municipal level. The ecological fallacy, met in Chapter 1, is its inferential twin — an area-level association does not establish an individual-level one. Both are reasons to report the unit of analysis prominently rather than burying it in a methods note.

What to take away

  • The five subsystems differ less in composition than in response time, and that spread — days for the atmosphere, a millennium for the deep ocean — explains which consequences of a decision arrive within a political term.
  • The energy balance yields 255 K; the surface is 288 K; the 33 K gap is the greenhouse effect stated arithmetically, and the same equation prices an albedo change.
  • Three circulation cells per hemisphere impose the global wet–dry banding, and place the Philippines in the rising, convective branch.
  • Soil forms over centuries and erodes in hours, which is why it is treated as non-renewable and why erosion is benchmarked against formation rate.
  • Urbanising a catchment raises and advances the flood peak without any change in rainfall — an attribution problem that only spatial analysis can settle.
  • Vector and raster encode different theories of space; resolution decisions silently become ecological ones.
  • A coordinate means nothing without its datum and projection. Mixed frames are the commonest silent error in applied GIS, and equal-area projections are mandatory for area claims.
  • Classification without an accuracy assessment is an assertion. Report the confusion matrix, both accuracies and kappa — not a single headline percentage.
  • Weighted overlay embeds the analyst's values in its weights, so the deliverable must include the weights, their derivation and a sensitivity analysis.
